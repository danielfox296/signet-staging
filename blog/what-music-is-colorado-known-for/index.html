What music is Colorado known for?

Visitors expect John Denver and jam bands. What they find depends on the city. Here is what Denver, Boulder, Colorado Springs and the ski towns sound like, and who we’d put in front of your guests in each.

Signet Artists · November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Most people who ask this question are about to host something. A conference is coming to Denver, or a company is taking its people to a resort for three days, or a couple from Chicago is getting married outside Boulder. Half the guests have never been here, and the host wants the night to feel like Colorado.

Colorado is known for John Denver, bluegrass and jam bands. That’s what visitors expect to hear. What they’d hear if they went out on a weeknight depends on the city: neo-soul, funk and jazz in Denver, sound baths and DJs in Boulder, horn players and hard rock in Colorado Springs, and the most appreciative crowds in the state up in the ski towns.

What do visitors expect Colorado to sound like? #

John Denver. The name carries the whole argument. “Rocky Mountain High” has been an official state song since 2007, and for a lot of people that’s where the picture stops.

Past that, they expect jam bands. The Grateful Dead and Phish have a huge following here, and Colorado legalized pot early. Leftover Salmon, The String Cheese Incident and Yonder Mountain String Band took bluegrass and stretched it into long improvised sets, and people outside the state started treating that as the Colorado sound. Those bands wander. At an event with a schedule, it’s easy to get lost in it.

The bluegrass underneath it is real. The Telluride Bluegrass Festival started in June 1974, and Hot Rize, out of Boulder, was the first bluegrass band from Colorado to tour the country. A trio on a lawn or a resort deck sounds like it belongs there, and guests can still talk. Our Americana band covers that ground, and we wrote about how Americana differs from strict bluegrass.

What does Denver sound like? #

The best of Denver right now is neo-soul with a mountain sensibility behind it. Think of the words to “Jolene” sung over D’Angelo’s chords.

Funk and jazz are all through it, and both have been here a long time. Welton Street in Five Points was called the Harlem of the West, and Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong and Count Basie played the lounge at the Rossonian Hotel when they came through. Three members of Earth, Wind & Fire went to East High School.

You can walk into it on a Wednesday night at Meadowlark, a basement bar on Larimer Street, where the guitarist Eman Alexander hosts a night called Off the Cuff. On Mondays the same bar has straight-ahead jazz, and that’s where the best players in town go to blow off steam after their weekend shows. Downtown, Dazzle hands its stage to the Blowing Loud Jam Sessions.

There’s a second Denver next to that one: outlaw Americana, and a roots sound close to Wilco, the way Austin has one. A studio in Golden called Clubhouse Recorders is the center of it, and the songwriting is the point. Thomas Owen is one of the best songwriters in that world. Meghan Clarisse won Folk/Americana Album of the Year at the 2026 Josie Music Awards, and she puts the singer out front. René Moffatt is a Texan, and he brought that with him. San Leandro takes Leo Albertoni’s songs and puts some of the best funk and fusion players in the city behind them.

The real Denver is not for everyone. Some hosts want background music or pop, and that’s a fine thing to want. For the ones who want their guests to hear the city, these are the names. From our own roster, we’d start with the funk band, the soul and R&B band or the Changes Jazz Trio. There’s more in What does Denver sound like?

What does Boulder sound like? #

Boulder looks more like Burning Man, or Bali. Sound baths, ecstatic dance, kirtan, electronic DJs. It’s its own small world of spirituality and tech, with a slight aversion to the Americana and roots sound the rest of Colorado loves.

The dance and kirtan side happens at places like the StarHouse, up Sunshine Canyon, and around Naropa University. For most visitors, Pearl Street still has the dining and entertainment that pleases a group without anyone having to search hard.

Boulder is also where the bigger touring acts stop, the way Red Rocks is for Denver, so less of what you hear there is home-grown. The home-grown part lives at the edges. Sarah Banker, up in Nederland, writes uplifting songs and means every word. Nederland also has the Ned Jazz & Wine Fest every August, a full day of music in a park by the creek, hosted by Stephanie Andelman and her crew.

When a group at a Boulder hotel like the St Julien or the Boulderado asks for something very Boulder, we pair a DJ with a live tabla player: electronic music with a world sound, in the Burning Man spirit. Our Afro house DJ covers that. A sound bath is the quieter version. If the group would rather have songs, it’s Chelsey Webb for high-energy twang, or Emily Barnes, whose songs hang in the air.

What does Colorado Springs sound like? #

The Springs is split down the middle.

One half is jazz. The Air Force Academy Band is based there, so there are a lot of horn players in town, and horn players love jazz. The Springs has a developed jazz scene, with players like Ricky Sweum, Todd Williams, Steve Langemo and Susan Rissman. You can hear it on Thursdays in the lobby of the Mining Exchange Hotel downtown, and the Todd Williams Trio plays great straight-ahead jazz. Jazz isn’t everyone’s taste. The area is rich in it.

For the local flavor, there’s Armadillo Ranch in Manitou Springs. Bluegrass is strong there too, with the mountains next door. The Broadmoor books polished, soothing talent.

The other half grew up on the loud, aggressive rock of the early 2000s and still loves it. The clubs cater to that crowd.

A corporate group in the Springs usually wants a clean, nostalgic party band. When people are there for work, they care less about hearing the city. So when we send a band down, it’s Quadrasonic, who come from the Vail Valley and play like they mean it, or our millennial party rock band. Our pages on the Broadmoor and Garden of the Gods Resort list the spaces and the acts we’d suggest for each.

What about the ski towns? #

The ski towns have the most appreciative crowds a Front Range band ever plays for. Everybody comes out and has a great time. At the high-end resorts you’re hearing top talent, whether it’s a cover band or a touring act. We wrote about evening ideas for a retreat in Vail.

One thing to check before you hire a soloist up there: can one person carry a big sound and keep the energy up? There’s a difference between an amateur with a guitar and a real performer, and a crowd that came out to have a great time will notice.

Should the music be authentic or familiar? #

That’s the planner’s call, and both are fine. If the night is meant to be immersive, we’d go authentic. If the music sits in the background, between conversations and the program, we’d go with nostalgia and songs people know. The second is safer.

Tejas Singh is a great safe choice: familiar songs, built up live with a looper. Roma Ransom, a violin and guitar duo, is the more artistic one. For a wedding that should feel like Colorado, Meghan Clarisse is the flexible answer. She has a little twang, and she can play the procession, the cocktail hour or a whole show. We go further into this in How to make a corporate event sound like Colorado.
