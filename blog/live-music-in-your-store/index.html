Live music in your store: when to book a band

A grand opening, a holiday open house or a customer appreciation night is a great time to bring a musician into your store. Here’s how to plan the music, and the licensing rule that comes with a live act.

Signet Artists · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • Live music is a great fit for an occasion in your store: a grand opening, a holiday open house, a customer appreciation night, or a loyalty event or trunk show. A performance gives people a reason to come in at a set time.
  • Recorded music is the easier choice for an ordinary day, since it plays at the tempo and volume you pick for every hour you’re open.
  • The radio exemption in 17 U.S.C. § 110(5) covers a broadcast, so it doesn’t cover a live act. ASCAP’s licensing FAQ says the business owner obtains the license for a performance in the business.
  • Before the event, settle the volume, the breaks, power and floor space, and the act itself.

Every so often your store has a reason for people to show up at a set time, rather than whenever they happen to walk by. It might be your grand opening, the holiday open house, or the evening you thank the customers who kept you busy all year. On those occasions, a musician in the store can make the whole thing feel like an event.

When is live music a good idea for a store? #

A performance has a start time. The music you play every day is on when the doors open and off when they lock, and nobody plans a Tuesday around it. A duo playing for two hours is an event, and an event is something you can put on an invitation.

Four occasions come up again and again. A grand opening is one, since your store doesn’t have regulars yet and live music is an easy way to make the first day feel like an occasion. The holiday open house is another. Everyone’s calendar is full in December, and a live musician gives people a reason to choose your store’s evening.

A customer appreciation night is for the people who already love your store, and live music is a lovely way to say thank you. Then there’s the loyalty event, the trunk show or the product launch, any evening you’ve invited people to come in for something special.

Does live music make a difference? #

There’s research behind the instinct. In a 2024 study published in PNAS, Wiebke Trost and colleagues had pianists play live for listeners lying in an MRI scanner, and compared the listeners’ response with recorded music. The live performances produced stronger and more consistent activity in the amygdala, the part of the brain that handles emotion, and the listeners’ brain activity locked to the music only when it was live. The pianists in that study were adjusting their playing to the listeners’ responses as they happened.

In a 2019 study at McMaster University’s LIVELab, published in Frontiers in Psychology, an audience at a live concert moved their heads faster than another audience did at a playback of the same band’s album. The people who called themselves fans moved the most, and everyone moved more when the music was live.

Both studies were a long way from a store, in a concert hall and a brain scanner, so we’d read them as a reason to bring in live music on an evening people are already coming in for. Learn more about how live and recorded music compare.

Should a store play recorded music on ordinary days? #

For most stores, yes. You’re open something like ten hours a day, and the people behind the register hear every minute of it. Recorded music plays at the tempo and volume you chose, for every hour the doors are open, and you can change it whenever you like.

Tempo can matter on an ordinary day. In a 1982 study in supermarkets, Ronald Milliman found that slower music slowed shoppers down and lifted daily sales. A live performance usually builds energy as it goes, which is perfect for a party in December and less of a fit for a quiet Tuesday afternoon in March.

That’s why most stores save live music for an occasion. Nordstrom once had pianists playing in most of its stores, and you can learn more about what happened to Nordstrom’s pianists.

Does the radio exemption cover a live band? #

No, and it’s the part that’s easy to miss. Once the band starts playing, the license question belongs to the store, whether anyone planned for it or not.

The exemption many small stores rely on, 17 U.S.C. § 110(5)(B), covers the communication of a transmission. That means a licensed radio or TV broadcast played in a store under 2,000 square feet, or in a larger store that stays inside the statute’s loudspeaker limits. A guitarist playing a Van Morrison song by your register is a public performance of a copyrighted song, and the exemption doesn’t cover her.

ASCAP’s licensing FAQ answers who holds the license. Some people assume the musicians have to get it. The law says everyone who takes part in a performance or is responsible for it is liable, and since the business owner gets the benefit of the performance, the business owner obtains the license.

So a band playing covers in your store can leave the store open to statutory damages under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c), unless your license already covers live performance or the act plays only music that no performing rights organization (PRO) represents.

Does your store need a license for the act you’re booking?

Ask the act two things: what they play, and whether their own songs are registered with a PRO. Covers of songs you’d hear on the radio are public performances of someone else’s work, and the store is the party the PROs license for them. Originals by a songwriter who’s an ASCAP or BMI member need the store’s license too.

Originals by a songwriter with no PRO membership are one of the two cases no PRO can collect on, and music in the public domain is the other. The radio exemption applies to none of these, since nothing is being broadcast.

How do you plan live music for a store event? #

Start with the volume. A store is a smaller space than most event venues, and an acoustic drum kit is loud in any store. A solo guitarist, a duo or a jazz trio without drums is a great fit, and we can keep the music at a level where nobody has to raise their voice to be heard. Learn more about how loud background music should be.

Next, plan the breaks. A band plays a set and then takes a break, so have your store’s own music ready to come on at a similar volume, and the store won’t go from a trio to silence all at once. Let the act know what will play when they stop, and bring your usual music back to its everyday level before the last customers leave.

Then there’s power and floor space. Ask the act how many outlets and how much floor they need, and how long they’d like to set up before the doors open.

Last comes the act itself. A musician who plays dinners and cocktail hours is used to playing at a level people can talk over, and that’s the kind of act we’d suggest for most store events.
