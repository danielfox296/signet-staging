For planners

Live music at Colorado's mountain venues.

You've found a beautiful venue in the mountains, and then someone mentions the noise cap. Here's what to know about noise caps, curfews, altitude and travel for a band playing Vail, Beaver Creek, Breckenridge, Aspen, Telluride or Steamboat. We've listed our sources for every number below, and we'll tell you where we couldn't confirm a town's rules.

Colorado's statewide noise limits

When people plan for volume, they usually look at the town's rules first. Colorado also caps noise statewide, and where the state number is the lower of the two, that's the number you have to meet. The Noise Abatement Act sets maximum levels measured 25 feet or more from the property line, and exceeding them is prima facie evidence (evidence that stands unless it's disproved) of a public nuisance.

Zone7am to 7pm7pm to 7am
Residential55 dB(A)50 dB(A)
Commercial60 dB(A)55 dB(A)
Light industrial70 dB(A)65 dB(A)
Industrial80 dB(A)75 dB(A)

Two details in the Act matter for an evening event. Between 7am and 7pm the limit rises by 10 dB(A) for up to fifteen minutes in any one hour, which is enough for a ceremony but not for dancing. And after 7pm, the limit in a residential zone is 50 dB(A) at 25 feet, which is quieter than a normal conversation. If your reception is at a private estate or ranch in a residential zone, that limit applies no matter how the band plays.

A town permit may not cover you

On September 8, 2025, the Colorado Supreme Court held that a municipality can't authorize a private for-profit venue to exceed the state caps. The 1987 amendment that exempts cultural and entertainment events applies to property used by a government body or a nonprofit, and the court read that narrowly. Salida could not issue amplified-sound permits for concerts at a commercial bar.

If your venue is a commercial property, a sound permit from the town may no longer settle the question. Ask the venue who owns the property and what exemption it falls under, and try to get the answer before you sign the contract.

What live music costs at a mountain venue

A resort event in Colorado usually costs more than a Denver event. The difference comes from travel, the extra day on the musicians' calendars, and lodging when the event runs too late for a sensible drive home on I-70.

Travel tiers

Front Range
This tier covers Boulder, Fort Collins and Colorado Springs, including the Broadmoor. These are day trips, so there's a small travel charge and no lodging, which makes the Broadmoor one of the better-value resort venues in Colorado for live music.
I-70 corridor
This tier covers Vail, Beaver Creek, Breckenridge and Winter Park. These events add a travel charge. The band can often drive back the same day if you finish early, but a late curfew usually means a night's lodging.
Far resorts
This tier covers Aspen, Telluride, Steamboat Springs and Crested Butte. These add a travel charge, plus a night's lodging, which we bill at cost. The band will need two days for these, whatever your run of show looks like.

A holiday weekend usually costs more than a standard date anywhere in Colorado. Curious what live music costs in Colorado more broadly? Our pricing page and the cost guide can help. The town matters as much as the date in the mountains, so please send us both.

Noise limits by mountain town

We checked these rules on August 7, 2026. Municipal codes change, and several of these towns were actively revising their noise rules when we looked. Treat this as a starting point for your conversation with your venue.

Aspen
Of all these towns, Aspen has some of the strictest rules and some of the clearest. Outdoor amplified sound at a private event is capped at 80 dBA at the perimeter, between 9am and 8pm, and a public event with a Special Event Permit gets 100 dBA until 9pm. Neighbors within 250 feet get seven days’ notice, and a property with a noise variance can hold two events a week. With an 8pm outdoor cutoff, plan the loudest part of the night indoors or earlier in the day.
Telluride
Amplified sound, indoors and out, can run 7am to 11pm on Fridays, Saturdays and holidays. It can’t be plainly audible beyond the limits the permit sets, and the permit application asks for expected decibel levels at set distances, which we can help you answer.
Breckenridge
Breckenridge’s reported limits are lower. In residential zones it’s 55 dB(A) from 7:01am to 10:59pm and 50 dB(A) outside those hours, and 70 and 65 in commercial zones, and the town manager can allow more at their discretion. We couldn’t reach the code text directly, so please check with your venue.
Steamboat Springs
Steamboat's reported limit is 60 dB(A) from 7am to 7pm and 55 dB(A) from 7pm to 7am. Amplified sound in a public place needs permission from the city manager.
Vail
Vail’s reported limit is 65 dB(A) outdoors in the resort villages. It’s measured ten feet from the permitted area, and the town manager can allow amplified sound for up to four days in a row. Vail has been reviewing these rules, so please confirm the current number with your venue.
Beaver Creek, Winter Park, Snowmass Village, Crested Butte, Mountain Village
We couldn't confirm the rules for these towns. Send us the venue and we'll get the answer from the town.

A town's published limit is measured at the property line or the perimeter, while a band can run 88 to 95 dBA on the dance floor. You can't compare those two numbers directly, because sound drops as it travels from the band to the property line. That distance depends on your site, which is why we'd like the venue name early.

Altitude

Denver sits at 5,280 feet and the corridor runs well above it. Breckenridge is the highest of these at roughly 9,600 feet, Mountain Village above Telluride is about 9,545, Winter Park about 9,052, Telluride 8,750, Crested Butte 8,909, Vail 8,150, Beaver Creek near 8,100, Snowmass Village 8,104, Aspen 7,908 and Steamboat 6,732.

That affects your run of show in two ways. Singers and horn players tire faster in thin, dry air, so a band playing four hours at 9,600 feet will pace itself differently than it would in Denver. And the temperature swing between sound check and the last set is wider up there than it is on the Front Range, which means more retuning across the evening.

That's why we'd build a longer sound check into your run of show and ask for water on the stage.

Travel and lodging

We charge lodging for the far resorts because of I-70 and the mountain passes. A drive that takes two hours in September takes considerably longer on a February Sunday, and a band that has driven six hours to Telluride isn't driving home the same night. If your event is in a town like Vail or Breckenridge and you'd rather we price it without lodging and make the drive, just tell us and we'll look at it with you.

Independence Pass closes in winter, so the drive from Denver to Aspen goes the long way around through Glenwood Springs.

Four questions for a mountain venue

Try to ask them before you sign the contract. If the noise cap or the curfew only comes up the week of the event, it could move the loudest part of your event or end it early.

  • What is the cap, as a number, and is it measured at the property line or at the perimeter of the event?
  • What time does outdoor amplified sound have to stop, and does it differ by day of the week?
  • Who holds the sound permit, and is the property government, nonprofit or commercial?
  • How far is the stage from the nearest property line?

Send us the venue name and we'll ask all four ourselves.
